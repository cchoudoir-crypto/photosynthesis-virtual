📏 How the two measurements work

O₂ production: count the O₂ bubbles released in 1 minute. Each bubble holds about 4 µL of O₂ ≈ 0.16 µmol O₂.
O₂ rate (µmol/min) = bubbles × 0.16

CO₂ consumption: a dissolved‑CO₂ probe in the 25 mL of water reads CO₂ at the start and end of the trial. CO₂ has a molar mass of 44 g/mol.
CO₂ rate (µmol/min) = (start − end, mg/L) × 0.025 L ÷ 44 × 1000

A negative CO₂ consumption means CO₂ increased: respiration released more than photosynthesis used.

Change only your independent variable between trials. Keep everything else the same. Run at least 3 trials at each setting.

Analysis questions
  1. Identify your independent variable, dependent variable, and at least three controlled variables.
  2. Describe the trend in your graph. Where does the rate increase, level off, or decrease?
  3. Why were the bubble counts slightly different for repeated trials at the same setting? Why does averaging multiple trials make your results more reliable?
  4. What gas is in the bubbles? Write the balanced equation for photosynthesis and circle the product you measured.
  5. Light: Why does the rate stop increasing at high light intensity? What becomes the limiting factor? Why are there no bubbles at very low light even though the plant is alive?
  6. Temperature: Explain the shape of the temperature curve using the idea of enzymes (e.g., RuBisCO). What happens to enzymes above the optimum temperature?
  7. CO₂: Which stage of photosynthesis uses CO₂? Explain why adding more baking soda increases the rate only up to a point.
  8. Compare your O₂ production and CO₂ consumption rates. What is the approximate ratio? Use the balanced equation (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) to explain why.
  9. Run a trial at 0% light. What happened to the CO₂ level, and why were there no O₂ bubbles? Which process was the plant still carrying out?
  10. Color: Which light color produced the lowest rate? Relate this to the color the plant appears and to the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll.
  11. Write a claim–evidence–reasoning (CER) conclusion that answers your research question. Was your hypothesis supported?
Teacher notes — how the model works
  • Net O₂ bubbles = gross photosynthesis − respiration (bubbles can't go below zero, so very dim light gives 0 — the light compensation point).
  • Light follows a saturation curve (rate levels off near 60–100%). Temperature follows an enzyme curve with an optimum near 30 °C, steeper drop above it, near 0 by ~45 °C. CO₂ (NaHCO₃) follows a saturation curve; 0% still gives a small rate from dissolved CO₂. Color: red ≈ white > blue > green (green is mostly reflected).
  • Two dependent variables: O₂ produced (bubbles × 0.16 µmol) and CO₂ consumed (probe drop converted to µmol, 25 mL water). Both track net photosynthesis at roughly a 1:1 molar ratio. In the dark or at extreme temperatures, CO₂ consumption goes negative (respiration) while bubbles stay at 0 — good for discussing the compensation point.
  • Bubbles are released randomly (a Poisson process), so repeated trials vary naturally — good for discussing reliability and averaging. Students get a warning if they change a controlled variable mid‑experiment.
  • Default conditions: 60% light, 25 °C, 0.5% NaHCO₃, white light. Manual counting mode works best at real‑time speed.
